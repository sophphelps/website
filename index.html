About Me

My journey into astrophysics began in ninth grade when *Cosmos* sparked an interest that would shape my future. I completed two years of Virginia Space Grant Consortium courses to deepen this fascination.

As a first-generation student, I navigated the college process independently. I built a spreadsheet tracking deadlines and requirements for every school and asked teachers for targeted essay feedback. The system kept me on schedule and organized.

After college submissions, my home became unsafe. I left due to domestic violence, which led me to couch surf, yet I still graduated valedictorian with a 4.0. At UCLA, dorm stability let me install a study and sleep routine I still use today.

The next summer, an eviction notice arrived a week after I returned home. My father had sold the house and emptied the joint account. We were homeless. I stayed with friends most nights and sometimes slept in my car. I worked about 50 hours per week (≈40 restaurant, ≈10 tutoring). I built a scheduling system to prevent overlaps and protect study time. After saving up for a deposit, my family and I moved to an apartment in California to start fresh.

Because my mother had been a homemaker and my brother was nine, I continued working to support us. Sophomore year was classes plus nearly full-time work. The issue was bandwidth, not interest. I kept coursework afloat by starting assignments the day they were posted, maintaining an agenda, and blocking focused study hours. My tutoring grew to 20–40 students weekly. I approached students as a peer, shared session notes, and wrote LaTeX study guides with acronyms, analogies, and flow charts so complex topics made sense. Families stayed multiple years and reported higher grades and test scores.

By junior year I needed to change my involvement in the field. With bandwidth limited and grades tied to family employment, I switched to physics and joined Professor Chris Regan's ferroelectrics group. I used nondimensionalization to shrink the design space and cut experiments, adapting a published method to model polarization switching in hafnium zirconium oxide (HZO) thin films and to interpret STEM-EBIC imaging for reliability. I documented decisions weekly and presented results at group meetings.

When time finally opened, *Planets and Exoplanets* with Erik Petigura gave me the sustained attention I had been missing and confirmed the fit. Exoplanets were a new lens, and I built time-series analyses and plots in Python for class projects. An Astrobites assignment on V1298 Tau, work my future mentor had contributed to, made the science click.

That course led to a summer at JPL with Gautam Vasisht, later extended through the academic year. I owned the reduction pipeline: I automated run order, documented usage, and was one of three who could run Stellar-GFIT. With MATLAB and bash I calibrated and quality-checked roughly 20,000 spectra and generated about 10,000 periodograms. I also ran the instrument during data collection and wrote the documentation the team used throughout the observing campaign.

This path left me with stronger problem-solving and patience, and with habits that travel: rigorous notebooks, disciplined time management, and clear communication. Exoplanet science moves quickly; careful pipelines and documentation unlock new signals others can trust. I plan to build reliable exoplanet pipelines and to teach students to do the same.
